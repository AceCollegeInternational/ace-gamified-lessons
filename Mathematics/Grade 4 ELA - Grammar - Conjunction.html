What Is a Conjunction?

A joining word that connects words, phrases, or whole sentences

A conjunction is a word that CONNECTS other words, phrases, or clauses.
Think of it like a bridge 🌉 — it holds two sides together!

📌 Conjunctions connect at three levels:

cats
and
dogs
← connecting WORDS
in the park
or
at the beach
← connecting PHRASES
She was tired
but
she kept going
← connecting CLAUSES
Three types of conjunctions:
1️⃣ Coordinating — join equal parts (FANBOYS: For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So)
2️⃣ Subordinating — join a main idea to a dependent idea (because, when, although…)
3️⃣ Correlative — work in pairs (either…or, neither…nor, both…and)

👆 Click each dotted word to reveal the conjunction!

I wanted to play outside, but it was raining.
Because she studied hard, she passed the test.
Either you can have cake or ice cream.
💡 Memory Hook: A conjunction is a JUNCTION — like a road junction where two streets meet! 🚦

Coordinating Conjunctions

The FANBOYS — 7 words that join EQUAL parts of a sentence

Coordinating conjunctions join things of equal importance:
two words, two phrases, or two complete sentences.
Remember them with FANBOYS! 🎸
FFor
AAnd
NNor
BBut
OOr
YYet
SSo
ConjunctionWhat it showsExample
ForReasonShe rested, for she was tired.
AndAdditionI like cats and dogs.
NorNegative additionHe can't swim, nor can he float.
ButContrastShe tried, but she failed.
OrChoice/alternativeYou can run or walk.
YetUnexpected contrastHe is tired, yet he keeps going.
SoResultIt rained, so we stayed inside.
⚠️ Comma Rule: Use a comma before a FANBOYS conjunction when it joins TWO complete sentences.
✅ "I was cold, so I wore a coat."  |  ❌ "I wore a coat and scarf." (no comma — joining two words only)

🔄 Flip cards — test your knowledge:

Which FANBOYS conjunction shows a RESULT or consequence?
✅ SO — "It snowed heavily, so school was cancelled." The result of heavy snow = cancelled school.
What's the difference between BUT and YET?
Both show contrast. YET has a stronger sense of surprise: "She never trains, yet she always wins!" — unexpected!

Subordinating Conjunctions

Words that add a DEPENDENT idea to the main sentence

A subordinating conjunction connects a main clause to a dependent clause.
The dependent clause cannot stand alone — it needs the main clause to make sense!
She passed the test
because
she studied hard

↑ Main clause         ↑ Subordinating conj.         ↑ Dependent clause

📦 Grouped by what they show:

⏰ TIME
when · while · after · before · until · since · as soon as
💡 CAUSE / REASON
because · since · as
⚡ CONTRAST
although · even though · though · while · whereas
🎯 CONDITION
if · unless · provided that · as long as
⚠️ Comma Rule for Subordinating Conjunctions:
✅ Comma when the subordinate clause comes FIRST:
   "Because it was raining, we stayed inside."
✅ No comma when the main clause comes FIRST:
   "We stayed inside because it was raining."

Subordinating in Action

Spot the conjunction · understand the relationship · check the comma

👆 Click to reveal the subordinating conjunctions:

Although the team lost, they played brilliantly. [Contrast — comma after the subordinate clause]
She put on a coat because it was cold outside. [Cause — main clause first, no comma]
When the bell rings, the students leave class. [Time — comma after the subordinate clause]
You won't pass unless you study the material. [Condition — main clause first, no comma]

🔄 Flip — test your understanding:

"Unless" belongs to which group of subordinating conjunctions?
✅ CONDITION — "Unless" means "except if." It sets a condition: "You can't go unless you finish first."
Complete: "Because I was late, ___" OR "___ because I was late" — which needs a comma?
✅ The FIRST version needs a comma. When "because" starts the sentence, the dependent clause comes first → add a comma before the main clause!

Correlative Conjunctions

Conjunction PAIRS that always work together — two parts, one connection

Correlative conjunctions always come in pairs.
The first part sets up the sentence; the second part completes it. They're like a matching set! 🧤

🔗 The main correlative pairs:

either
or
→ "Either you study, or you fail."
neither
nor
→ "Neither Jack nor Jill was there."
both
and
→ "Both Maya and her brother won."
not only
but also
→ "She is not only smart but also kind."
whether
or
→ "Whether it rains or shines, we'll be there."
⚠️ Remember the pair rule:
NEVER use only one half! ❌ "Neither Jack was there." (missing "nor")
✅ "Neither Jack nor Jill was there."
Why do we call them CORRELATIVE conjunctions?
✅ "Correlative" comes from "correlate" — to have a mutual relationship. These conjunctions relate to each other; they must always appear together as a team! 🤝

Conjunctions in Action!

Choosing the RIGHT conjunction for the RIGHT relationship

Different conjunctions show different relationships between ideas.
Choose wisely — the wrong conjunction can change the whole meaning!
RelationshipConjunctions to useExample
➕ Addition and, both…and, not only…but also She sings and dances.
⚡ Contrast but, yet, although, even though, however I was tired, but I kept going.
💡 Cause because, since, as, for I smiled because I won.
🎯 Result so, so that, therefore It rained, so we went inside.
⏰ Time when, while, before, after, until I read while she cooked.
🔮 Condition if, unless, as long as You can go if you finish first.
🔀 Choice or, either…or, whether…or Would you like tea or coffee?
💡 Sentence Combining Power:
Weak: "It rained. We stayed inside." (choppy!)
Strong: "Because it rained, we stayed inside." (connected, flowing!)

Combining Sentences

See how the right conjunction changes the meaning — click to reveal each version!

Two sentences:   "I was nervous."   +   "I gave a great speech."

How does the conjunction change the meaning?

I was nervous, and I gave a great speech. ← Addition (just lists both facts)
I was nervous, but I gave a great speech. ← Contrast (surprising result!)
Although I was nervous, I gave a great speech. ← Contrast (same meaning, subordinating style)
I was nervous, so I practised more before I gave my speech. ← Result (nervousness caused extra practice)
How do you know which conjunction to pick when combining sentences?
✅ Ask: What is the RELATIONSHIP between the two ideas? Is it contrast? Addition? Cause? Result? The relationship tells you which conjunction to use!
💡 MCAP tip: Questions often ask you to combine two sentences. Always identify the relationship first, THEN pick your conjunction!
